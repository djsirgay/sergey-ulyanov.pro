Listen first.

One short learning sequence connects a text lesson to the working sound library, Audio Lab, and Coach. No video or account is needed.

Prototype learning sequence written by Sergey Ulyanov for discussion with Louis Colaianni. It is not a published or Louis-approved course, an excerpt from his books, or evidence of measured learning gains.

Text lesson · 01

Hear a person, not a category.

A country or first-language label is a starting point for finding a recording—not a rule for how everyone in that group speaks.

  1. Open Discover. Choose a GMU sample and read its source information.
  2. Add it to Audio Lab. Listen once without trying to imitate.
  3. Listen again. Choose one short phrase and describe what you hear: a vowel, an /r/, a rhythm, or a pause.

“In this recording, I notice…” is more useful than “People from this place always…”

Use GMU’s shared passage for the comparison in step 02. IDEA samples open on their original website.

Text lesson · 02

Same words. Different choices.

Add a second GMU voice to Audio Lab. Both speakers read the same elicitation passage, so you can return to the same words.

  1. Play the phrase “Please call Stella” in each recording. Use the loop controls if helpful.
  2. Describe one difference and one similarity. Keep the sample names and source links with your notes.
  3. Separate an audible observation from a possible explanation. If you are unsure, write a question for expert review.

Compare these two recordings—not entire populations. There is no “correct accent” score here.

If the Lab is empty, add two GMU references from Discover first. External playback depends on the archive.

Text lesson · 03

Choose a target. Keep your agency.

Choose one feature you want to explore for a specific role or communication task. The aim is an intentional choice, not erasing your own voice.

  1. In Coach, select a reference and write your teaching or practice task.
  2. Make a short drill. If you want to record a take, Audio Lab keeps it local; recording is optional.
  3. Write what you tried, what you noticed, and one question for Louis. Keep any expert feedback separate and ask before recording or quoting it.

“What changed in this phrase—and what would I like to try next?”

A self-rating is optional and subjective, not a measured learning outcome. Export a session if you need a backup.

Coach Workspace

Document a teaching task, try a source, and keep observations separate from expert feedback.

Expert walkthrough · before you start
  1. Ask permission before keeping comments; do not record the meeting without separate permission.
  2. Let the expert choose one real teaching task and try it. Note where help was needed.
  3. Keep observed actions, expert opinions, and your proposed changes distinct.
  4. Agree on up to three next changes and, separately, whether the expert may be named publicly.

Prototype review, not a learning-outcomes study. Notes stay in this browser; export a backup. A share link includes coaching notes—review them before sharing.

Why this exists

Archives are evidence.
A workflow makes them usable.

Joy of Phonetics is not an accent generator and does not score whether a voice is “correct.” It helps actors, coaches, and researchers move from a primary-source speaker to documented listening, practice, and annotation.

IDEA

Roughly 1,800 primary-source accent and dialect samples from around the world, with biographical details, transcriptions, and commentary on many records.

Method

Each speaker is an exemplar, never a proxy for a nation or social group. Observation, source commentary, inference, and coaching hypothesis should remain distinct.
